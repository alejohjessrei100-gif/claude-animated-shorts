## Facts (source: Wikipedia, "Leaning Tower of Pisa"; Burland et al., Pisa stabilisation reports)
- Construction began 1173. By 1178, with the 3rd floor built, the tower began to sink on one side:
  soft ground of clay, fine sand and shells, and a foundation only about 3 m deep.
- Work stopped for almost a century because Pisa was at war (Genoa, Lucca, Florence). The pause let the soil
  settle and consolidate; engineers say without it the tower would almost certainly have toppled.
- Work resumed 1272 (Giovanni di Simone): upper floors built taller on one side to compensate, so the tower is curved.
  Bell chamber finished 1372. Height ~56 m, weight ~14,500 tonnes.
- 1934: Mussolini ordered it set upright; holes were drilled into the foundation and filled with grout/concrete.
  The tower sank further and the lean got worse.
- 1990-2001: closed; engineers removed soil from under the north (high) side (soil extraction). Lean cut from about
  5.5 deg to about 4 deg, roughly 45 cm at the top; judged stable for at least 200 years.
- It was deliberately not straightened fully: the lean was kept for its heritage and tourist value.

## Script
The most famous tower in Italy started falling over before they'd even finished the third floor.
It's 1178 in Pisa, and the town is building a marble bell tower to show off to every city around.
Three floors up, the builders notice something: one side of the whole thing is quietly sinking into the ground.
Then a war breaks out, everybody drops their tools, and the tower is left standing there, leaning, for almost a hundred years.
Luckily, those hundred years gave the ground time to settle.
Unluckily, the next builders took that as permission to keep going.
Their plan: make every new floor a little taller on the short side.
So now the tower doesn't just lean. It bends, like a banana.
You see, the tower is standing on soft clay and sand, on a foundation only three metres deep.
Ever stood on a mattress on one foot? The more you lean, the more that side sinks, and the more you lean.
That's fourteen thousand tonnes of marble, standing on one foot.
In 1934, Mussolini ordered it straightened, so workers drilled holes in the base and pumped in concrete.
The tower sank even further.
In the nineties, engineers finally saved it by gently scooping soil out from under the high side.
They could have made it perfectly straight.
They didn't, because nobody flies to Italy to take a photo holding up a tower that's standing perfectly fine.
