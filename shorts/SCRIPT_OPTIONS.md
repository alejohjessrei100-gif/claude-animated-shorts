## 01 · The Warship That Sank in the Harbour (Vasa, 1628)
Facts (Wikipedia "Vasa (ship)", Vasa Museum): 64 bronze guns on two gun decks, ~500 carved sculptures; pre-launch heel test
(30 men running across the deck) stopped after 3 runs because she rocked so badly; launched anyway 10 Aug 1628; sank ~1,300 m out
when a gust heeled her and water entered the open lower gunports; too little ballast for the weight up high; master shipwright
Henrik Hybertsson had died in 1627; inquiry punished nobody; raised 1961 (333 years); Vasa Museum, 1M+ visitors a year.
Light: day (launch) → golden (sinking) → day (museum).

Sweden built the most powerful warship in the world, and it sank before it even left the harbour.
It's 1628, and the king wants a ship so big and so heavily armed that every navy in Europe will panic.
So the Vasa gets sixty-four bronze cannons, two whole decks of them, and hundreds of carved, painted statues.
Before launch, an admiral runs a test: thirty sailors sprint from one side of the deck to the other.
After three runs, the ship is rocking so hard he stops the test before it tips over.
And then, they launch it anyway.
Thousands of people line the shore to wave it off.
It sails about thirteen hundred metres, a gust of wind leans it over, water pours in through the open gun ports, and down it goes.
You see, a ship stays upright because its heavy stuff sits low.
Ever stood up in a canoe? The higher your weight, the less it takes to tip you over.
The Vasa had tonnes of cannons up high, and not nearly enough heavy rock down in the bottom.
The king demands that somebody gets punished. The trouble is, the shipbuilder died the year before.
Three hundred and thirty-three years later, Sweden lifts it out of the mud, almost perfectly preserved.
Today it's the most visited museum in Scandinavia.
So the ship that only managed thirteen hundred metres now gets over a million visitors a year.

## 02 · The Exploding Whale (Florence, Oregon, 1970)
Facts (Wikipedia "Exploding whale"; KATU news film, 12 Nov 1970): ~45 ft, ~8-ton sperm whale on the beach; beaches were run by the
Oregon Highway Division; engineer George Thornton used half a ton (20 cases) of dynamite, expecting scavengers to clear the bits;
explosives-trained veteran Walter Umenhofer warned 20 sticks would be enough; spectators a quarter mile back; blubber rained on
the crowd and cars; gulls stayed away; most of the whale remained and was buried; a large chunk crushed Umenhofer's new Oldsmobile,
bought at a dealer promotion called "Get a Whale of a Deal". No one was hurt.
Light: day throughout (overcast-bright beach), golden for the ending.

In 1970, Oregon decided the best way to get rid of a dead whale was to blow it up.
A forty-five foot sperm whale has washed up on the beach in Florence, and it's starting to smell.
The beach belongs to the highway department, so the job lands on a highway engineer.
His plan: half a ton of dynamite. The seagulls can clean up the little pieces.
An old army explosives man in the crowd says that's way too much. Twenty sticks would do it, not twenty boxes.
Nobody listens. The crowd moves back a quarter of a mile, a TV crew starts filming, and the engineer presses the button.
The whale goes up. Then the whale comes down.
Chunks of blubber rain over the crowd, the cars and the dunes, and the seagulls are too scared to come anywhere near it.
You see, an explosion doesn't make anything disappear. It just makes it move very fast.
Ever stomped on a ketchup packet? The ketchup doesn't vanish. It just ends up on everything.
And most of the whale is still lying on the beach, so the crew has to bury it anyway.
One huge chunk flattens the roof of a parked car.
It belongs to the explosives man who warned them.
He'd just bought it, at a car dealer's sale called Get a Whale of a Deal.

## 03 · The Jet That Ran Out of Fuel (The Gimli Glider, 1983)
Facts (Wikipedia "Gimli Glider"; Canadian Board of Inquiry): Air Canada Flight 143, Boeing 767, 23 July 1983, 61 passengers + 8 crew;
fuel gauges failed; the fleet's first metric aircraft; manual calculation used 1.77 (lb/L) instead of 0.8 (kg/L), so it carried
less than half the fuel needed; both engines flamed out at 41,000 ft over Manitoba; Captain Bob Pearson was an experienced glider
pilot; First Officer Maurice Quintal had served at RCAF Station Gimli, by then partly a drag strip hosting a family race day;
forward slip to lose height; nose gear collapsed; all 69 survived, minor injuries; pilots were demoted/suspended, then received
the FAI Diploma for Outstanding Airmanship (1985); the plane flew until 2008.
Light: day (cruise) → golden (glide and landing).

In 1983, a brand new passenger jet ran out of fuel at forty-one thousand feet, and every single person survived.
It's Air Canada's shiniest plane, a Boeing seven sixty-seven, and the fuel gauges aren't working.
No problem: the crew measures the fuel by hand and works out how much it weighs.
Halfway across Canada, a warning beeps. Then another. Then both engines go quiet, and most of the cockpit screens go black.
They're now flying a hundred-tonne glider with sixty-nine people on board.
Luckily, the captain flies gliders as a hobby.
Unluckily, the nearest runway is an old air force base, and today it's hosting a family car race.
He tips the jet sideways to burn off height, the nose wheel collapses, and it scrapes to a stop just short of the picnic.
You see, Canada was switching to metric, and this was the airline's first plane that measured fuel in kilograms.
But the crew's sums used pounds.
Ever followed a recipe that just says two hundred of flour? Grams or ounces, you could be way off.
A pound is less than half a kilo, so the plane took off with less than half the fuel it needed.
First, the airline punished both pilots.
Then they got an international award, for some of the best flying anyone had ever seen.

## 04 · The Great Stink (London, 1858)
Facts (Wikipedia "Great Stink", "Joseph Bazalgette"; Halliday, The Great Stink of London): hot summer 1858; Thames carried untreated
sewage of ~2.5M people, worsened by flush toilets; Parliament's curtains soaked in chloride of lime (bleaching powder); MPs
discussed moving out; after years of delay a bill passed in 18 days; Bazalgette built ~1,100 miles of street sewers and 82 miles of
intercepting sewers, sizing pipes about double the need ("we're only going to do this once"); still in use; the cause of cholera
was dirty water, not smell (miasma theory was wrong), but the sewers ended London's cholera outbreaks anyway.
Light: hot golden haze (stink) → day (sewer works) → day (twist).

In 1858, London got so smelly that Parliament finally did something.
It's the hottest summer anyone remembers, and the River Thames is basically one long open toilet.
Two and a half million people's waste flows straight into it, and the new flushing toilets are making it worse.
The stink rolls into the Houses of Parliament, right on the riverbank.
The politicians soak their curtains in bleach. They talk about moving Parliament out of London.
After years of arguing about sewers, they pass a law in eighteen days.
You see, heat makes bacteria eat faster, and the faster they eat, the more stinky gas they let off.
Ever left a bin bag outside in August? Same thing, only this bin bag was a river.
An engineer called Joseph Bazalgette designs over a thousand miles of new sewers, carrying everything far downstream.
Then he makes the pipes twice as wide as they need to be, because they're only going to do this once.
Those pipes are still carrying London's waste today.
But here's the thing. Everyone thought disease came from the smell.
It didn't. Cholera came from the dirty water.
So Parliament built the sewers to fix the smell, and accidentally stopped cholera instead.

## 05 · The Monk Who Hated Bubbles (Champagne, late 1600s)
Facts (Wikipedia "Champagne", "Dom Pérignon"; Kladstrup, Champagne): the region's cold winters stopped fermentation; it restarted in
spring in the bottle, making CO2; bottles exploded in chain reactions, cellar workers wore iron masks; "le vin du diable"; Dom
Pérignon (1638-1715), cellar master at Hautvillers Abbey, worked to avoid refermentation (bubbles were a fault); the English bought
the wine, liked the fizz, had stronger coal-fired glass, and Christopher Merret described adding sugar to make wine sparkle (1662);
the "Come quickly, I am tasting the stars!" line first appears in a late-1800s advertisement.
Light: cool cellar (lamp pools) → day (vineyard) → golden (ending).

The monk famous for inventing champagne actually spent years trying to get rid of the bubbles.
It's the late sixteen hundreds in Champagne, France, and Dom Pérignon is in charge of the wine at his abbey.
His wine has a problem. Every spring, down in the cellar, the bottles start exploding.
One bottle bursts, the glass hits the next one, and the whole rack goes off like popcorn.
Some years, a cellar loses a fifth of its bottles. The workers go downstairs wearing iron masks.
People call it the devil's wine.
Dom Pérignon spends his life trying to make it calm and still, like a respectable wine.
You see, Champagne is so cold that the yeast falls asleep in winter before it's finished eating the sugar.
In spring it wakes up inside the sealed bottle and keeps making gas, with nowhere for it to go.
Ever shaken a bottle of soda? Now imagine it shaking itself, for weeks.
Meanwhile, the English are buying the same wine, and they love the fizz.
Their coal-fired glass is strong enough to hold it, so they add sugar on purpose to make even more.
The French eventually give in, build stronger bottles, and make the fizz their thing.
And that famous line where Dom Pérignon shouts, come quickly, I'm tasting the stars?
It was written by an advertiser, more than a hundred and fifty years after he died.

## 06 · The Melted Chocolate Bar (the microwave oven, 1945)
Facts (Wikipedia "Percy Spencer", "Microwave oven"; Smithsonian): Raytheon engineer Percy Spencer, working near an active magnetron
for radar, found a candy bar melted in his pocket; tested popcorn kernels (popped) and an egg (exploded, spattering a colleague);
patent filed 1945; first Radarange (1947) about 1.8 m tall, ~340 kg, ~$5,000, water-cooled; Spencer left school around age 12;
Raytheon paid him its standard token $2 patent bonus.
Light: lab interior with warm lamp pools → day (kitchen ending).

The microwave oven was invented because a man's chocolate bar melted in his pocket.
It's 1945, and Percy Spencer is building radar sets for the war, standing right next to a glowing magnetron tube.
He reaches into his pocket for a snack and finds a warm, gooey mess.
Most people would just wash their trousers. Percy sends out for a bag of popcorn.
He holds it next to the tube, and it starts popping all over the lab.
Next, he tries an egg. A curious coworker leans in for a closer look, and the egg explodes in his face.
You see, the tube sends out invisible microwaves, and they grab every water molecule in the food and twist it back and forth, billions of times a second.
Ever rubbed your hands together on a cold day? All that rubbing turns into heat. Microwaves do that inside the food.
Two years later, his company sells the first microwave oven.
It's as tall as a fridge, weighs as much as a piano, and costs about as much as three new cars.
Today there's one in most kitchens in the world.
Percy never even finished school.
And for the patent, his company gave him the standard bonus: two dollars.

## 07 · The Skyscraper That Melted a Car (20 Fenchurch Street, London, 2013)
Facts (Wikipedia "20 Fenchurch Street"; BBC/Guardian Sept 2013; LA Times on Vdara 2010): ~160 m tower by Rafael Viñoly, concave south
face; summer 2013 it focused sunlight on Eastcheap; parts of Martin Lindsay's Jaguar XJ melted (mirror, badge, panels), developers
paid for repairs; a shop's doormat/carpet was scorched and tiles cracked; reporters fried an egg in the hot spot; nicknames
"Walkie-Scorchie"/"Fryscraper"; fixed with a permanent sunshade of fins (2014); Viñoly's curved Vdara hotel in Las Vegas had
focused sun on its pool deck, singeing a guest's hair and melting plastic ("Vdara death ray").
Light: day (hard sun) throughout, golden for the Las Vegas beat.

In 2013, a London skyscraper melted a parked car from across the street.
The tower is brand new, a hundred and sixty metres tall, and its front curves out like a giant glass spoon.
Locals call it the Walkie-Talkie.
One sunny afternoon, a man comes back to his Jaguar and finds the mirror drooping, the badge sagging, and the panels warped.
Across the road, a shop's doormat starts to smoke, and the floor tiles crack.
A reporter goes down with a frying pan and an egg, and fries it right there in the street.
You see, that curved glass face works exactly like a giant mirror dish.
It catches the sunlight hitting the whole building and bounces it into one hot spot down on the street.
Ever burned a leaf with a magnifying glass? Same trick, only the glass is a hundred and sixty metres tall.
The developers pay for the car, and bolt a huge sunshade onto the tower.
Then someone remembers the architect's last big curved building.
A glass hotel in Las Vegas that focused sunlight onto its own swimming pool, and singed a guest's hair.
The newspapers called that one the Death Ray.
London called theirs the Walkie-Scorchie.

## 08 · The Aircraft Carrier Made of Ice (Project Habakkuk, 1942-44)
Facts (Wikipedia "Project Habakkuk", "Pykrete"; Perutz's account): Geoffrey Pyke proposed an ice carrier for the mid-Atlantic air gap
against U-boats, with steel short; pykrete = water + ~14 % sawdust/wood pulp, frozen, far tougher and slower to melt than ice;
planned ship ~600 m long; Mountbatten fired a pistol at ice (shattered) and pykrete (bullet ricocheted past the officers) at the
1943 Quebec Conference; test model built on Patricia Lake, Jasper, Alberta, kept frozen with refrigeration; cancelled as aircraft
range, Azores bases and steel supply improved; with cooling off, the model took three summers to melt.
Light: cold day (ocean / lab) → day (lake) → golden (melting summers).

During the Second World War, Britain seriously tried to build an aircraft carrier out of ice.
It's 1942, German submarines are sinking ships in the middle of the Atlantic, and steel is running out.
An inventor called Geoffrey Pyke has an idea: icebergs don't sink, and the ocean is full of free ice.
So why not carve out a floating runway, six hundred metres long?
There's one problem. Ice is brittle. One torpedo, and it shatters.
So Pyke's team mixes sawdust into the water before freezing it, and the new stuff is shockingly tough.
They name it after him: Pykrete.
At a meeting of top commanders, Lord Mountbatten pulls out a pistol and shoots a block of normal ice. It shatters.
Then he shoots a block of Pykrete. The bullet bounces off and whizzes past the officers' legs.
You see, the wood fibres work like a net inside the ice. When a crack starts, the fibres hold it shut.
Ever wondered why old mud bricks have straw in them? Same idea.
They build a test model on a lake in Canada, and it floats there all summer.
But planes get longer range, steel comes back, and the whole project is cancelled.
The workers switch off the freezers and leave the model to melt.
It takes three whole summers.

## 09 · The Morning a Country Changed Sides (Dagen H, Sweden, 1967)
Facts (Wikipedia "Dagen H"): Sunday 3 Sept 1967 Sweden switched from left- to right-hand traffic; neighbours drove right and most
Swedish cars were already left-hand drive; 1955 referendum: 82.9 % voted no; ~360,000 signs changed; all non-essential traffic
banned overnight, vehicles had to stop at 04:50, change sides carefully, and restart at 05:00; accidents and insurance claims fell
markedly afterwards; within about two years rates returned to normal (attributed to drivers' extra caution, risk compensation).
Light: night-blue → dawn golden (the switch) → day.

In 1967, an entire country switched to driving on the other side of the road, in one morning.
Sweden drives on the left. All its neighbours drive on the right, and most Swedish cars have the steering wheel on the left anyway.
Twelve years earlier, the Swedes voted on switching. Eighty-three percent said no.
The government does it anyway.
Overnight, workers swap three hundred and sixty thousand road signs. Every car is told to stop at ten to five in the morning.
Then, on the signal, the entire country slowly drives across to the right, waits, and sets off again at five.
Everyone expects chaos. The ambulances are ready.
Instead, crashes drop. A lot.
You see, when people feel safe, they drive like they're safe. When they're nervous, they slow down and actually look.
Ever walked across an icy car park? You don't slip on the ice you're watching. You slip on the bit you didn't see.
For months, a whole nation drives like the road is made of ice.
Then they get used to it.
Within two years, the crash numbers are right back where they started.
So the safest thing Sweden ever did for its roads was confuse everybody.

## 10 · The War the Birds Won (The Great Emu War, 1932)
Facts (Wikipedia "Emu War"; Australian Army records): ~20,000 emus raiding wheat farms around Campion, Western Australia, many farmed
by ex-servicemen; Defence Minister Pearce sent Major G.P.W. Meredith, two soldiers, two Lewis guns, 10,000 rounds (Nov-Dec 1932);
emus scattered into small groups; an ambush of ~1,000 birds ended when a gun jammed after about 12; a gun mounted on a truck failed
because emus (up to ~50 km/h) outran it on rough ground and the gunner couldn't aim; withdrawn after about a month; Meredith: a
division with "the bullet-carrying capacity of these birds" could face any army; later fences and bounties worked better; emus
number in the hundreds of thousands today.
Light: day (hot wheat fields) throughout, golden for the retreat.

In 1932, the Australian army went to war against birds, and the birds won.
Twenty thousand emus have marched into the wheat fields of Western Australia, and they're eating everything.
The farmers, many of them old soldiers, ask for help. The army sends a major, two soldiers, and two machine guns.
On day one, they find a flock of about fifty emus and open fire.
The emus scatter in every direction, and the gunners hit almost nothing.
A few days later, they ambush a thousand birds. The gun jams after just twelve.
So they bolt a machine gun onto a truck and give chase.
But an emu can run fifty kilometres an hour over rough ground. The truck can't, and the gunner can't even aim.
You see, a machine gun works best on a big crowd standing still.
Emus don't stand still. They split up, every bird running its own way.
Ever chased pigeons in a park? Run at the flock, and it turns into twenty tiny flocks.
After a month, the army goes home.
The major says that with soldiers as tough as these birds, he could face any army in the world.
In the end, the farmers beat the emus with something much simpler: fences.
