During World War Two, America built a guided missile, and the pilots were pigeons.
It's 1943, and psychologist B. F. Skinner is training pigeons to peck at a picture of an enemy ship.
Every good peck gets a seed.
He straps three of them into the nose of a bomb, each facing a tiny screen.
The generals laugh. "Pigeons? Really?"
But in the tests, the birds keep the bomb locked on target.
Wait, how does a bird steer a bomb?
You see, a lens shows the target on the screen.
When it drifts, the pecks tilt the screen, and that steers the bomb.
And if one pigeon gets it wrong, the other two outvote it.
Ever trained a dog with treats? Same idea, but with explosives.
The military cancelled it anyway.
Skinner said the problem was that nobody took them seriously.
Eighty years later, he was finally given a prize for it: the Ig Nobel Peace Prize.
