## 01 · Why Toast Lands Butter-Side Down
Facts: physicist Robert Matthews, "Tumbling toast, Murphy's Law and the fundamental constants", European Journal of Physics
(1995); Ig Nobel Prize in Physics 1996. Toast sliding off a table edge picks up spin and only completes about half a turn
before hitting the floor from normal table height; for a full turn tables would have to be about 3 m high; he linked the
limit on human (and so table) height to fundamental constants.
Scenes: kitchen at golden morning light, toast slices mid-air, a physicist on a ladder, a giant family at a 3 m table.

A physicist proved your toast lands butter-side down because of how tall you are.
It's 1995, and Robert Matthews is pushing buttered toast off tables.
His colleagues think he's lost it. "It's just bad luck."
But the toast keeps landing butter-down.
Wait, how does toast know which side has the butter?
You see, as the toast tips off the edge, it starts to spin.
From a normal table, it only has time for half a turn before it hits the floor.
Ever caught a flipped coin too early? Same problem.
For toast to land butter-up, tables would need to be three metres tall.
So the only real fix is being a giant.

## 02 · The Most Expensive Sick Flower
Facts: Dutch tulip mania peaked in early 1637 (Amsterdam / Haarlem); the "broken" striped tulips such as Semper Augustus
were the most prized, with one bulb quoted around 10,000 guilders, often compared to the price of a canal house.
The stripes came from tulip breaking virus, spread by aphids, which also weakens the bulb. (Mike Dash, Tulipomania; Wikipedia "Tulip mania", "Tulip breaking virus")
Scenes: candle-lit Amsterdam tavern auction, a single bulb under a glass dome, aphids as tiny cartoon bugs, prices on chalk boards (no text: coins piling up).

In 1637, people paid the price of a house for a flower that was actually sick.
It's Amsterdam, and the hottest thing around is a tulip with flaming red and white stripes.
"I'll give you my house for that bulb." "Deal!"
Prices double, then double again. Then suddenly, nobody's buying.
Wait, why were the striped ones so special?
You see, those stripes came from a virus, spread by tiny bugs called aphids.
Ever splashed bleach on a dark shirt? The colour drops out in streaks.
The virus did that to the petals, and it made the bulbs weaker.
So the most valuable flower in history was a tulip with the flu.

## 03 · The Pigeons Who Flew a Bomb
Facts: Project Pigeon (1940-44), psychologist B. F. Skinner, funded by the US National Defense Research Committee. Pigeons
trained with food rewards to peck at the image of a target projected on a screen in a missile's nose cone; three pigeons
per missile; pecks off-centre steered it. Tests went well; cancelled in 1944. Skinner: "our problem was no one would take
us seriously." Revived as Project Orcon (1948-53), cancelled again. (Wikipedia "Project Pigeon"; Skinner 1960, American Psychologist)
Scenes: lab with pigeon cages and warm desk lamps, pigeons in tiny pilot caps (cartoon), generals laughing round a table, test range.

In World War Two, America built a guided missile, and the pilots were pigeons.
Psychologist B. F. Skinner trains pigeons to peck at a picture of an enemy ship.
Every good peck gets a seed.
He packs three of them into the nose of a bomb.
The generals laugh. "Pigeons? Really?"
But in the tests, the pigeons keep it on target.
Wait, how does a bird steer a bomb?
You see, a lens shows the target on a little screen, and when it drifts, the pecks pull it back.
Ever trained a dog with treats? Same idea.
The project was cancelled anyway.
Skinner said the problem was nobody took it seriously.

## 04 · Mpemba's Physics
Facts: Erasto Mpemba, Tanzania, 1963: making ice cream at school, put his hot milk-and-sugar mix in the freezer and it froze
before the cooler mixes. A teacher told him "that is Mpemba's physics and not the universal physics". In 1969 he and
visiting physicist Denis Osborne published the result; the "Mpemba effect" is still debated (proposed causes: evaporation,
convection, dissolved gases, supercooling). (Mpemba & Osborne, Physics Education 1969; Wikipedia "Mpemba effect")
Scenes: bright school kitchen, a freezer packed with bowls, a classroom laughing, a lecture hall, steaming bowls racing.

A schoolboy proved hot water can freeze faster than cold, and got laughed at.
It's 1963 in Tanzania, and Erasto Mpemba is making ice cream.
The freezer's nearly full, so he shoves his mix in hot.
It freezes first.
His teacher: "That's not physics. That's Mpemba's physics."
Years later, a professor tests it. It's true.
Wait, how can hot water win the race?
You see, hot water loses more of itself as steam, and it stirs itself as it cools.
Ever watched a hot bath fog a mirror? Water escapes.
Today it's called the Mpemba effect, and scientists still argue why.
The class joke ended up with his name on it.

## 05 · The Wrong-Way Pilot
Facts: Douglas Corrigan, 17-18 July 1938: denied permission for a transatlantic flight in his modified 1929 Curtiss Robin,
he filed a plan from Floyd Bennett Field, New York, to California, flew east instead and landed at Baldonnel, Dublin,
after about 28 hours. He blamed a compass error (said he followed the wrong end of the needle in poor light) and never
admitted otherwise. His licence was suspended for 14 days, the time it took his ship home; New York's parade for him drew
a crowd reported larger than Lindbergh's. (Wikipedia "Douglas Corrigan")
Scenes: hangar at night, a patched old plane, clouds and ocean, an Irish airfield at dawn, ticker-tape parade street.

In 1938, a pilot flew to Ireland by accident, and America threw him a parade.
Douglas Corrigan wants to fly across the Atlantic, but officials say his plane's too old.
So he files a flight plan home to California.
He takes off from New York into the clouds, and twenty-eight hours later, lands in Dublin.
"Where am I?" "Ireland." "Huh. Must be my compass."
Wait, can you really miss California by a whole ocean?
You see, he said he followed the wrong end of the compass needle.
His punishment? Fourteen days without a licence, how long his boat home took.
And he never, ever admitted it was on purpose.

## 06 · Rome's Pee Tax
Facts: Roman fullers (laundries) collected urine from public pots; aged urine produces ammonia, used to clean and whiten
wool. Emperor Vespasian (r. 69-79) taxed urine collection; Suetonius says his son Titus objected, and Vespasian held a coin
to his nose and asked if it smelled ("pecunia non olet", money doesn't stink). Public urinals in France were called
"vespasiennes". (Suetonius, Vespasian 23; Wikipedia "Urine tax", "Fuller")
Scenes: steamy Roman laundry with tubs, workers stomping togas, a golden palace hall, a coin close-up, a Paris street urinal.

Ancient Rome had a tax on pee, and it made the emperor rich.
It's the year 70, and Rome's laundries are collecting urine from pots all over the city.
Why? They wash togas in it.
Workers stomp the clothes in tubs of pee all day long.
Then Emperor Vespasian says, "Great. Let's tax it."
His own son complains it's disgusting.
Vespasian holds a gold coin under his nose. "Does it smell?"
Wait, why would pee clean anything?
You see, old urine turns into ammonia, which breaks down grease and dirt.
Check your cleaning cupboard. Ammonia's still in there.
And in France, public urinals were named after him.

## 07 · Nobody Wanted the Shopping Cart
Facts: Sylvan Goldman, owner of Humpty Dumpty supermarkets in Oklahoma City, introduced the folding shopping cart in June 1937
(inspired by a folding chair, two baskets, wheels). Shoppers ignored it: men said they were strong enough to carry baskets,
women said they had pushed enough baby carriages. He hired people to push carts around the store as if shopping, and use
took off; he became a multi-millionaire. (Smithsonian Magazine; Oklahoma Historical Society; Wikipedia "Sylvan Goldman")
Scenes: 1930s grocery with warm lamps and stacked tins, a folding chair on wheels, actors pushing carts, a crowded checkout.

When the shopping cart was invented, nobody wanted to push one.
In 1937, grocer Sylvan Goldman sees shoppers quit when their baskets get heavy.
So he bolts wheels onto a folding chair and stacks two baskets on top.
He fills the store with them. Nobody touches them.
Men say, "I can carry my own groceries." Women say, "I've pushed enough baby carriages."
Wait, so how did carts take over the world?
You see, Goldman hires people to push carts, pretending to shop.
Ever skipped an empty restaurant for a busy one? People copy people.
Within weeks, everyone's pushing a cart.
Goldman got rich by paying people to pretend to buy groceries.

## 08 · The Dancing Plague
Facts: Strasbourg, July 1518: a woman (Frau Troffea) began dancing in the street; within weeks dozens, later reportedly
hundreds, joined. Physicians and the city council prescribed more dancing, built a wooden stage and hired musicians; it
spread. The council then banned public music and dancing and sent dancers to the shrine of St Vitus. Leading explanation:
mass psychogenic illness amid famine and disease. (John Waller, A Time to Dance, A Time to Die, 2008; Wikipedia "Dancing plague of 1518")
Scenes: medieval street at dusk with torches, a council hall, a wooden stage with pipers and drummers, a mountain shrine.

In 1518, a whole city caught something that made people dance.
It's Strasbourg, and a woman named Frau Troffea starts dancing in the street. For days.
Soon, dozens join her.
The city council has a plan. "They need to dance it out."
So they build a stage and hire a band.
Wait, did that work?
No. Even more people joined in.
You see, historians think it was panic spreading from person to person, in a year of hunger and fear.
Ever yawned just because someone else did? Brains copy brains.
In the end, the city banned music and dancing.
So the cure for the dancing plague was no more dancing.

## 09 · The First Speeding Ticket
Facts: 28 January 1896, Paddock Wood, Kent: Walter Arnold drove at about 8 mph where the limit was 2 mph (Locomotive Acts,
written for steam traction engines; the 1865 Act required a person walking ahead with a red flag, relaxed in 1878 to a person
walking ahead). A policeman chased him on a bicycle. He was fined one shilling plus costs. The Locomotives on Highways Act
1896 raised the limit to 14 mph from 14 November 1896, celebrated with the "Emancipation Run" from London to Brighton, still
held yearly as the London to Brighton Veteran Car Run. (Wikipedia "Walter Arnold", "London to Brighton Veteran Car Run")
Scenes: Victorian village street, a sputtering early car, a bobby on a bicycle pedalling hard, a man with a red flag, a jubilant road race.

The first speeding ticket in history was for driving eight miles an hour.
It's 1896 in England, and Walter Arnold is zooming through a village in his brand new motor car.
The speed limit? Two miles an hour.
A policeman spots him, and gives chase. On a bicycle.
He catches him.
Wait, why was the limit slower than walking?
You see, the law was written for steam engines, and cars used to need a man walking in front with a red flag.
Arnold gets fined a shilling.
Months later, the limit jumps to fourteen, and drivers celebrate with a race to Brighton.
That race still runs every single year.

## 10 · Napoleon Wasn't Short
Facts: Napoleon's height at his 1821 autopsy was recorded as 5 pieds 2 pouces in French units, about 1.69 m (5 ft 7 in in
English units), average or slightly above for a Frenchman of his time. British caricaturists (James Gillray's "Little Boney")
drew him tiny and tantrum-prone; his troops' nickname "le petit caporal" was affectionate. The "Napoleon complex" is named
after him. (Wikipedia "Napoleon complex", "Napoleon"; Gillray caricatures, British Museum)
Scenes: candle-lit French field tent, a doctor with a measuring tape, a London print shop full of cartoon prints (pictures only), two rulers side by side.

History's most famous short man was actually average height.
It's the 1800s, and Napoleon is conquering half of Europe.
His doctor writes down his height: five foot two.
British cartoonists love it. They draw him tiny, stomping his feet, throwing tantrums.
Wait, five foot two is short, isn't it?
You see, that was in French inches, which are longer than British ones.
Ever bought clothes from another country? Same size number, totally different fit.
In British inches, he was about five foot seven, normal for his time.
But the cartoons won.
Two hundred years later, we still have a complex named after him.
