In this town, every time someone talks over you, your voice gets a little quieter. Interrupt someone at dinner and they lose a whisper of sound. Get talked over all through school and by graduation you sound like a mouse. Loud people stay loud forever. And nobody was quieter than Juni. She had three older brothers, a teacher who never called on her, and friends who finished her sentences for her. By age eleven, her voice was so soft that people leaned in and still heard nothing. Kids called her Mute Juni. They'd ask her questions just to laugh at her lips moving. The only one who waited for her was her grandpa. He always told her the same thing. The quiet ones hear everything. Then one winter, a school bus slid off the mountain road and disappeared into deep snow. Every adult in town ran up the hill with shovels, screaming the kids' names. But the louder they yelled, the more the snow above them cracked and slid. The sheriff ordered everyone to stop shouting. Now they couldn't call out, and they couldn't hear a thing over the wind. Juni walked into the middle of the field and closed her eyes. She'd spent eleven years listening to everyone else. So she listened. And under all that snow, she heard it. A tiny tapping, like a pencil on glass. She pointed. They dug. And there was the bus, every kid inside. That night, the whole town went silent when Juni spoke. Not because they had to. Because they finally wanted to hear her.
